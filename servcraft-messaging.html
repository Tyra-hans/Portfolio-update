Messages, Redesigned
for Speed and Ease

Field service management · Communications · B2B SaaS

My role Product designer
Platform Web app
Status Shipped · monitoring

For field service businesses, every job runs on communication — a customer needs to know their technician is on the way, a team needs to know a job is booked. When ServCraft made email a paid add-on, the question sharpened: was this experience good enough to charge for, or was a confusing screen costing customers time and confidence every time someone hit send? That question became the catalyst for fixing years of design and tech debt.

Context

The Pricing Change That Made This Non-Negotiable

Messaging isn't a side feature for ServCraft's customers — it's how their business runs. A job gets created, a status changes, a technician goes en route: each of those moments fires a message, automatically, through a template, to a customer or a teammate. Get that wrong and it's not just a UI problem — it's a business represented poorly, a customer left uncertain, a team out of sync. The messaging feature had never had a designer look at it. When email became a paid add-on, it needed one.

The 2-month timeline reflects the full project span. The design work itself was more focused, but competing priorities, stability work, and other business demands forced several pauses and de-prioritisations along the way before this shipped.

Baseline Research

I pulled session recordings from Clarity across customers with different usage levels to establish a baseline: what was working, what was being avoided, what patterns had formed.

01

Templates: well-loved

One sweeping motion: load template, review, send. Faster users did it in a single flow. This had to stay intact.

02

Send: easy to use

Users reached it naturally and completed the action. Worth protecting.

03

Communication is core

Messaging is central to how ServCraft customers run their business. Customer data showed averages of 1,000 to 3,000 emails sent monthly across segments — this is a high-use, high-stakes feature.

04

Mixed compose behaviour

A significant number of sessions showed users typing messages from scratch rather than using templates. Templates were faster, but freeform composing was a real pattern that the redesign had to support without friction.

Two separate contact pickers to work through when adding recipients. Three different places to insert a replacement tag, each behaving slightly differently depending on where you were on the screen. A formatting toolbar carrying more options than the flow ever needed. None of it was broken on its own — but stacked together, it slowed people down.

Breaking Down the Screen

I annotated every element of the compose screen. Six issues stood out:

  • Recipient section: two always-open panels with no clear hierarchy, likely slowing users down
  • Load Template button isolated from the compose area
  • Replacement tags far from the text toolbar, and the double curly bracket syntax is not user-friendly
  • SMS Credits buried and disconnected from the send flow
  • Attachment area lacked clear state and a distinct action to add files

The Redesign

Many teams had built deep habits around the existing flow — habits built on trusting that the message would look right and reach the right person. Each change had to earn that same trust: from new users learning the flow for the first time, and from established teams who relied on it every day to keep their business running and their customers informed.

01

Replacement tags: from brackets to something human

Curly bracket syntax reached long-term technical users. Green pill tags triggered by @ follow a pattern familiar from messaging apps, bringing personalisation to the same depth, accessible to everyone.

02

Contact selector: one field instead of many

Customers and employees lived in separate always-open panels that dominated the screen. A single field with search surfaces job-relevant contacts first. Faster to reach, easier to confirm.

Reflection

What This Project Taught Me

1

Baseline before redesign. Session data gathered first made it possible to separate signal from noise and measure whether the redesign worked.

2

Pricing pressure is a legitimate design brief. "We're charging for this now" sharpened scope and gave the team a shared reason to prioritise.

3

Scope discipline is a design skill. Naming what's deferred — and why — keeps the backlog honest and the team moving.

4

Tech debt in the UI is a user experience problem. Unlabelled elements and mismatched fields erode trust and signal a product built for speed, not for the person using it.
